How to lower cortisol without changing jobs.

It's what powers your morning: the trouble starts when it stays switched on late into the night.

In this article

  1. 01In the morning, you need it
  2. 02When it stops coming down
  3. 03What keeps it switched on
  4. 04What brings it down

If you have 30 seconds

  1. 1In the morning you need cortisol: it's what gets you going.
  2. 2The problem is when it's still high at night: you sleep badly and wake up flat.
  3. 3You put it back on schedule with morning light and an evening that winds down.

It's half past eleven. You've sent the last email and you're in bed, shattered. Yet your mind is racing as if it were mid-morning, and by four you're wide awake, rehearsing tomorrow's meeting.

Behind all this there's almost always a hormone that nobody has sent home: cortisol. Mind you, it isn't the enemy. In fact, you can't do without it. The problem is when it's still on duty at eleven at night.

In the morning, you need it

Cortisol gets you going. After the overnight fast it raises your blood sugar and gives you the energy to start: it peaks about half an hour after you wake up. At night, though, it needs to stay low, because that's the shift for deep sleep and for growth hormone, which repairs your tissues.

Think of it as your best colleague in a crisis: you want them in the meeting, not in your bedroom at midnight, reopening files.

The right curve

High when you wake, easing down through the day, at its lowest around midnight. The habits below protect it.

When it stops coming down

After months under pressure, this often happens: in the morning cortisol is normal, but during the day it barely falls, and in the evening it's still up. You're tired but can't switch off, and the next morning you wake up flat, just when you need the push. So you add a coffee, you push on, and by evening you're wired again. It's a vicious circle.

If this carries on, your body pays the price: your memory slips, your muscles respond less to training, your blood pressure rises. If these signs have been with you for weeks, talk to your doctor as well: it helps rule out other causes.

What keeps it switched on

  • The packed evening. Emails at eleven, tense calls after dinner: stress that arrives late pushes cortisol up when it should be falling, and the next morning you wake up drained.
  • The afternoon coffee. The 4pm one takes hours to clear: it's still in your system when you switch off the light.
  • The skipped lunch. When the fuel runs out, your body uses cortisol itself to push your blood sugar back up.
  • Short nights. One five-hour night is enough: the next day you have more cortisol in circulation and handle sugar less well.
  • The drink to unwind. It feels like a brake, but in the evening alcohol breaks up your sleep: you sleep, but you recover less.

Your cortisol is just out of sync.

What brings it down

You can't switch off the pressure of your job. Your daily timings, for the most part, are up to you.

Light, as soon as you're up. The same alarm seven days a week and, as soon as you're up, ten minutes outside. Here in Gran Canaria it's easy, I know, but it works in London in November too: even under a grey sky, there's more light outside than in any office. It's the signal that starts cortisol at the right time and, about sixteen hours later, brings on sleep.

Coffee in the right window. In the morning it works with cortisol, which is already high on its own. Have your last one straight after lunch. If even one makes you jittery, cut back in the morning too.

Your breath, on demand. It's the only stress lever you can pull whenever you like, even in a lift. Breathe in through your nose for four seconds and out slowly for six, five times: the long exhale puts the brakes on your nervous system.

Proper breaks. Every hour, two minutes on your feet. Take calls on the move when you don't need a screen, and after lunch, walk for ten minutes.

Train with judgement. Moving clears stress like few other things. But if you're already under pressure, ninety gruelling minutes at nine at night is extra load, not a release. Keep sessions under an hour, the hardest ones in daylight.

An evening that winds down. In the last hour, no emails, and low, warm lights. At dinner, don't cut out carbohydrates altogether: a sensible portion helps many people sleep better.
