How to sleep well, even in a hotel.

A few simple habits to take with you on your next business trip.

If you have 30 seconds

  1. 1On your first night somewhere new you sleep more lightly: that's normal, and it usually gets easier from the second night.
  2. 2Darkness, a room at 18 to 20°C, a light dinner and living on local time: you can control all of these anywhere.
  3. 3Your evening routine, packed in your suitcase, matters more than the perfect room.

The work dinner ends at midnight and the alarm goes off at six, in an unfamiliar room. I hear this a lot from the people I coach: at home they sleep, on the road they spend half the night staring at the ceiling.

Away from home you can't control everything. But you can control enough: light, temperature, timing, dinner, screens and your evening routine. That's what goes in your suitcase, along with a spare shirt.

On the first night, your brain keeps watch

If you sleep worse on your first night in a hotel, it's not a sign of weakness. In a new place, part of your brain stays on alert, like a night watchman, and any door slamming in the corridor can wake you. Your sleep stays lighter, and from the second night it usually gets better.

Knowing this saves you from the most common mistake: getting wound up and checking the time every twenty minutes. And you can plan ahead when you book, by asking for a room away from the lift and the street.

Dark and cool, even far from home

Light is the strongest signal your body clock receives. At night you need proper darkness: curtains fully drawn and, if light still creeps in, an eye mask. Watch out for the little standby lights on the TV and the air conditioning too: even a tiny light can disrupt melatonin, the hormone that tells your body it's night.

Then there's temperature. To fall asleep your body needs to cool down a little, and hotel rooms are often kept warm. As soon as you walk in, set the thermostat to between 18 and 20°C. Before bed, take a warm shower, not a scalding one: when you step out, your body sheds heat, and that drop is exactly what brings sleep on.

With jet lag, light is in charge

When you cross several time zones, your body clock stays on home time. It catches up, but at its own pace: allow almost a day for every hour of difference. And for the same number of hours, flying east is harder, because your body stretches its day more willingly than it shortens it.

  • If you fly east, look for sunlight in the morning. If you fly west, look for it at the end of the day.
  • From day one, switch to local time: eat when the locals eat, and keep coffee to the first part of the day.
  • If you can, don't schedule the big meeting for the day you land.

Late dinners and that extra glass

Work dinners start late and finish later. You can't always move them, but you choose what's on your plate: grilled fish or meat with vegetables, without emptying the bread basket, and no fried food or mature cheeses. In the evening your liver and gut struggle more with fats and sugars, and a heavy dinner keeps your heart rate up just when it should be coming down.

Alcohol plays a trick on you: it knocks you out sooner, then breaks up the second half of the night. One glass, not three. And the five o'clock coffee, the one that gets you through the last meeting, is still half in your system at eleven at night.

Your evening routine goes in the suitcase

If you have an evening routine at home, bring it with you: your herbal tea, the book you're reading. If you can't sleep properly without your own pillow, bring that too. Your body gets ready for sleep through familiar habits, even in an unfamiliar room.

In the last hour, no emails and no box sets: set your alarm before dinner and leave your phone on the desk, away from the bed. The light from the screen tells your brain it's still daytime.

And if you're still awake after twenty minutes, don't wrestle with the pillow. Get up, keep the light low, read a few pages in the armchair, and go back to bed when sleep comes.

The room belongs to the hotel. The last hour before lights out belongs to you.

If you sleep badly at home too

These tips are for nights away. If you sleep badly in your own bed as well, and have done for weeks, the hotel isn't the problem: talk to your doctor. The same goes for melatonin: it's a hormone, so whether and how to take it is their call.
